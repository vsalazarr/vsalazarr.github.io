Working Papers

Does Conservation Work in General Equilibrium?

with Gabriel Leite-Mariante · Draft: July 2026

Paper

Abstract

Deforestation for agriculture accounts for roughly a fifth of global CO2-equivalent emissions. Because most forest conservation policies are spatially targeted, their local gains can be undone in general equilibrium if the deforestation they prevent simply relocates-leakage. We quantify leakage for two flagship Brazilian policies, the Priority List and Protected Areas, using an open-economy quantitative spatial model in which costly deforestation endogenously supplies agricultural land, inverted on regional data and disciplined by micro estimates and reduced-form policy effects. Leakage is small: over 2003-2018, general-equilibrium adjustment undoes 3.7-8.8% of the deforestation avoided by the Priority List and 6.4-14.7% of that avoided by Protected Areas, and less in carbon terms. What makes leakage small is where conservation is targeted. Restricting regions where agricultural land is valuable leaves cheap suppliers available to absorb displaced demand; restricting regions where land is cheap to clear removes the cheapest suppliers, leaving residual supply steep and inducing intensification rather than relocation. Brazilian deforestation is predominantly supply-driven, explaining both low leakage and why the Priority List leaks less. This mechanism yields a model-free diagnostic of leakage risk: targeted regions' share of the market value of newly cleared land relative to their share of cleared area.
